# Academic Writing

Isaac's prose rules. Apply when drafting or editing anything he will read, sign, or submit as academic work.

## The baseline

**Strunk & White's *The Elements of Style*** is the default. If a rule below does not cover the case, fall back to Strunk & White.

## Hard rules

### 1. Omit needless words

Every word earns its place. Before finalizing any sentence, try cutting each word. If the sentence still works, the word was dead weight.

- Bad: "In order to price the job correctly, I had to take into consideration the fact that..."
- Good: "To price the job, I considered..."

### 2. Strong verbs beat adverbs

A precise verb always beats a weak verb propped up by an adverb.

- Bad: greatly increases → Good: **surges**
- Bad: went down quickly → Good: **collapsed**
- Bad: said loudly → Good: **shouted**

Hunt adverbs ending in *-ly*. Most should die. The filler adverbs below should die on sight:

- *genuinely, precisely, fundamentally, entirely, remarkably, truly, profoundly, deeply, inherently, essentially, notably, significantly*
- Generic intensifier phrases built from them: *fundamentally reshaped, entirely new, truly significant, deeply rooted, profoundly shaped*

Either cut the adverb or pick a specific verb. "Fundamentally reshaped" is either "reshaped" or, if the action was larger, "overturned" / "inverted" / "rewrote."

**Exception:** "Unfortunately" is Isaac's own convention. Keep it where it reads naturally.

### 3. Active voice

The subject performs the verb. Passive voice hides the actor and bloats the sentence.

- Bad: "The price was set at fifty dollars by me."
- Good: "I set the price at fifty dollars."
- Bad: "It was deepened by the rain."
- Good: "The rain deepened it."

Passive voice is legitimate when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately downplayed. Otherwise, go active.

### 4. Direct over indirect

State the claim as the subject performing the verb, not as a wind-up phrase pointing at the claim.

- Bad: "What made Artusi's project succeed was the breadth of the regions it drew from."
- Good: "Artusi's project succeeded because it drew from every region of the peninsula."

Wind-up phrases ("What X is..." / "The reason X is..." / "It is the case that X...") usually exist to delay the real subject. Cut them and front-load the claim.

### 5. Remove AI conventions

These patterns signal machine-written prose. Scrub them.

- **Em dashes sparingly.** If a comma, colon, semicolon, or period does the job, use that. Reserve the em dash for the rare case where only it works — genuine parenthetical disruption or a hard swerve in thought.
- **Parentheses sparingly.** Same rule. If other punctuation works, use other punctuation. Parentheses are for genuine asides that break the sentence's frame.
- **No "It's not X, it's Y"** or its inverse ("Not just X — Y," "This isn't just A; it's B," "not merely X but Y"). Just state Y.
- **"Rather than X, Y" is the same tic.** It is slightly cleaner than "not X but Y," but it is still a negative-contrast construction. If it shows up in every paragraph, it becomes a tell of its own. Default to stating Y without needing to negate X. Reserve "rather than" for the case where the reader genuinely needs to see the contrast to understand Y. One or two uses across an essay is fine; one per paragraph is too many.
- **No "X existed, so Y followed."** This is lazy causal chaining. Show the mechanism: what about X produced Y, through what channel, under what conditions.
- **No hedging paragraph openers:** "Yet," "Despite," "Nevertheless," "Nonetheless." Prefer "However," "Although," "As such," "Thus," "From [angle]."
- **No throat-clearing or hedging phrases:**
  - "It's worth noting that..."
  - "It's important to remember..."
  - "In the realm of..."
  - "At the end of the day..."
  - "Needless to say..."
- **No quote-framing filler:** "captures X precisely," "observes astutely," "rightly notes." Use plain *writes*, *argues*, *notes*.
- **No filler vocabulary that signals LLM prose:**
  - *delve*, *tapestry*, *realm*, *journey* (as metaphor), *navigate* (as metaphor), *unpack* (as verb for analyze), *leverage* (as verb)
  - *showcase*, *pivotal*, *testament to*, *underscore* (when "show" works)
  - "on the other hand" chains as structural scaffolding
- **No symmetric three-part lists** that exist only for rhythm. If the third item was invented to balance the sentence, cut it.

### 6. Vary sentence length

The goal is rhythm, not fragments. Monotone writing, where every sentence is the same length and built the same way, signals AI prose. The fix is to mix lengths: a short sentence, then a longer one that builds the idea out, then a medium one to steady the rhythm, then a short one when a point needs landing.

**Short is not the same as fragmented.** A short sentence is still a complete sentence with a subject and a verb. "It worked." is short. "Turning work down." is a fragment and reads as either a caption or an AI tic. Fragments are legitimate as deliberate craft (dialogue, rhythmic emphasis), but default to complete sentences in academic prose.

**Do not chain short sentences.** Three or four short sentences in a row produces choppiness, which reads as broken prose rather than economical prose. If you find three consecutive short sentences, combine at least two of them with a semicolon, a comma with a conjunction, a colon, or subordination, and keep only one short for the landing.

**Longer sentences carry multiple clauses.** Isaac's own writing tends toward longer sentences that join clauses with *because*, *as such*, *thus*, *although*, *however*. Prefer logical subordination to lazy conjunction ("and... and... and...").

**Medium is the default, not long.** In Isaac's strongest prose (the Western Civ II research paper), most sentences run 20-30 words. Occasional synthesis sentences stretch to 40+, and occasional landing sentences drop below 15. Draft prose that fails density checks usually clusters in the 35-50 word range — every sentence technically fine, collectively exhausting. The cure is not to make every sentence short; it is to break the cluster by letting some sentences drop to 10-20 words while others extend.

## Paragraph structure

### Every paragraph opens with an analytical topic sentence

The topic sentence states the paragraph's claim. It must encompass the full breadth of what the paragraph covers. Details, evidence, numbers, and narrative follow — they never lead.

- GOOD: "Zicrone's pricing evolved through two regimes, and each regime worked because it matched the economics of my position rather than the economics of my competitors."
- BAD: "The first thing I had to do when I started Zicrone was set a price." (narrative throat-clearing, not an analytical claim)

The test: can a reader who only reads the topic sentences of every paragraph reconstruct the argument? If yes, the topic sentences are doing their job. If the topic sentences are mostly narrative ("First I did X. Then I did Y."), the argument is hiding in the details.

**Target length: 15-25 words, one clause.** Isaac's strongest topic sentences punch. "The dietary divide between Northern and Southern Italy mirrored deeper economic and social fractures across the peninsula." (16 words.) "Artusi's contribution to Italian unification was a cookbook that deliberately set out to forge a national cuisine from the diverse regional traditions of the peninsula." (25 words.) A topic sentence that stretches past 30 words and stacks two or three clauses usually contains two claims and signals the paragraph should split.

### One claim per paragraph, supported by evidence

Each paragraph makes one clear point and builds that point with the evidence that supports it. No stream-of-consciousness. No two-thirds-of-the-way-through pivots to a second point.

**Split when a paragraph develops a second analytical claim.** Test: does any sentence inside the paragraph read like it could be a topic sentence in its own right ("The cost side of the rule was more layered than it first sounded"; "The margin also moved with the rhythm of my schoolwork")? If yes, the paragraph is carrying two claims and should split at that pivot. A dense 500-word paragraph with three such pivots should become three paragraphs.

**Target length: 100-250 words per paragraph.** The Western Civ II paper runs paragraphs of that size consistently. Paragraphs of 400+ words almost always pack multiple analytical developments into one block and read as dense even when each sentence is fine on its own.

### Transitions bridge, not just pivot

The end of one paragraph should set up the beginning of the next. Good transitions build a logical bridge from the prior claim to the coming claim. "Yet" and "Despite" as cold paragraph openers usually signal a missing bridge.

### Conclusions wrap back

The closing paragraph should return explicitly to the thesis and the framing the essay opened with. The reader should feel the essay closing a loop, not trailing off.

## Academic register rules

### No contractions

Academic prose uses full forms. "Do not," not "don't." "It is," not "it's." "Would have," not "would've."

Apply this when the context is clearly academic (essays, papers, formal reports). Conversational registers keep contractions.

### Numbers (Turabian)

- **Spell out numbers below 100.** *Six years*, *forty minutes*, *three jobs*, *ninety-nine*.
- **Use numerals for 100 and above.** *100*, *1,800 words*, *2,500*.
- **Dollars stay as numerals with the symbol.** *$50*, *$200*, *$15 per hour*.
- **Percentages:** spell out "percent" in humanities-style Turabian prose. Numerals otherwise (*40 percent*).
- **Mixed expressions:** when one number in a sentence crosses 100, use numerals for all of them in that sentence for parallelism.

### Capitalization after colons

When a colon introduces a complete sentence, capitalize the first word of that sentence. When it introduces a list or phrase, lowercase.

- Complete sentence: "The rule was narrow: Take the next job only if its revenue beat the cost of the study time."
- Phrase or list: "The numbers were stark: 110 minutes for one job, 63 for three."

### Italics for titles

Italicize all book titles in body text per Turabian (*The Elements of Style*, *La scienza in cucina*).

### Citations

Cite only when using a specific fact that is not from the writer's experience or common knowledge. Do not pad a bibliography.

## Editing checklist

When reviewing prose (the writer's or another's), walk this list:

1. Read each sentence aloud in your head. Does every word earn its place? Cut the dead ones.
2. Circle every adverb. For each, ask: is there a stronger verb that replaces the verb+adverb pair? Scan for the filler-adverb list in particular.
3. Mark every passive construction. For each, ask: is the actor known and relevant? If yes, flip to active.
4. Read every paragraph's first sentence. Does each one make an analytical claim that covers the paragraph's full content? If the first sentence is narrative or throat-clearing, rewrite it.
5. Search for em dashes. For each, ask: would a comma, colon, semicolon, or period work? If yes, replace.
6. Search for parentheses. Same question. If yes, replace.
7. Scan for "not X, it's Y" constructions. Rewrite.
8. Scan for "X existed, so Y followed" and other lazy causal chains. Show the mechanism.
9. Scan for hedging paragraph openers ("Yet," "Despite," "Nevertheless"). Replace with "However," "Although," "As such," "Thus."
10. Scan for the filler vocabulary and quote-framing filler lists above. Replace with plain words.
11. Read three consecutive sentences. If they are all the same length, break the rhythm. If three or more are all short, combine at least two — chained shorts read as choppy, not economical.
12. Scan for fragments (sentences missing a subject or verb). In academic prose, default to complete sentences; reserve fragments for deliberate craft.
13. For academic contexts: scan for contractions (expand), numerals below 100 (spell out), and the word "percent" (spelled out in humanities prose).
14. Check the closing paragraph: does it return to the thesis and the opening framing?

## Isaac's voice notes (for narrative-analytical essays)

When writing or editing a first-person essay for Isaac, match these patterns beyond the general rules above.

### Ground the claim before analyzing it

After the analytical topic sentence, add concrete narrative texture: dates, places, mechanisms, the specific way something worked in practice. The pattern is claim → grounded detail → analytical development, not claim → immediate abstraction. Concrete texture is not padding; it earns the analytical moves that follow.

### Flowing, but not overloaded

Isaac's voice favors longer, integrative sentences with multiple clauses joined by *because*, *since*, *while*, *although*, *however*. Short landing sentences and one-beat paragraphs read as too punchy for his register.

That said, a sentence that stacks three levels of subordination (because → since → which meant) or chains four or more "and"-joined clauses becomes overloaded and should split. The test: read the sentence aloud in one breath. If it strains, split it at the next logical pivot. One flowing sentence beats three choppy ones; two medium sentences beat one overloaded one.

### "However" and "Instead" pivot within paragraphs

Use *However* for contrast and *Instead* for substitution. Both open a new sentence after a period as paragraph-internal pivots — no need to subordinate the pivot into a comma clause. Not *Yet*, not *Despite*, not *Nevertheless*.

### "However" is the preferred pivot

Use *However* (mid-sentence or paragraph-internal) as the primary contrast connector. Not *Yet*, not *Despite*, not *Nevertheless*.

### Adverbs that do real semantic work are fine

The filler-adverb list above stays banned, but a precise adverb doing specific work is welcome. Isaac will write "silently influenced," "held steady for four years," or similar when the adverb names a real quality. The test: could a stronger verb replace the verb+adverb pair without losing meaning? If yes, use the verb. If no, the adverb is earning its place.

### Integrative thesis sentences over bullet road maps

When previewing or closing an argument, weave the components into a thesis-restating sentence rather than listing them as "This paper walks through X, Y, Z." The reader should feel the argument structure through the sentence's grammar, not a signposted list.

### Terminology

Prefer *economic principles* and *economic concepts* to *economics* as a bare noun when referring to the body of ideas. *Economics* is fine as the discipline or the class.

### Concrete over formal-academic vocabulary

Words like *regime*, *paradigm*, *framework*, *apparatus*, *mechanism* (as filler) sound too academic for Isaac's voice. Replace with concrete or descriptive alternatives: *iteration*, *method*, *approach*, *pattern*, *setup*, or just describe the thing directly ("pay-per-hour method," "the way I set prices"). The test: does the word do real work, or is it academic-register scaffolding? If the latter, swap it out.

### Active agency — make the writer the actor

When the writer took an action, say so in the first person. Prefer "I chose $50 because..." to "The first rate was set at $50 because..." Prefer "allowing me to charge more" to "an inelastic buyer will pay more" when the writer is doing the charging. Abstract principle statements belong where the point is genuinely general; where the point is about what Isaac did, keep Isaac as the subject.

### Drop meta-commentary that the structure already carries

If a paragraph already demonstrates the paper's thesis through its analysis, do not restate the thesis in the paragraph's closing clause. "Even though every step has a name in the textbook" at the end of a pricing paragraph is restating a point the reader already has. Cut it. The structure does the work.

### Precision only where it serves

Use exact numbers and dates where they anchor the analysis. Use approximate language ("a few years," "around 2023," "roughly") where exactness is not load-bearing. Over-specifying where precision does not matter reads as academic posturing.

### Parallel construction — cut redundant repetition

"On my side... on my side," "held together... held together," naming a subject twice when pronoun works. Parallel structures should reuse the grammar, not the words. If the second instance of a word is not doing new work, cut it.

### Use concept terms directly, not as announcements

When naming an economic or technical concept a described action maps to, identify the action AS the term. Do not announce the term as a label the reader is being taught.

- WRONG: "**Marginal analysis** is the name for what I was doing, the practice of..."
- WRONG: "Economists call this **economies of density**."
- WRONG: "This phenomenon is what theory describes as **opportunity cost**."
- RIGHT: "That rule was **marginal analysis** in practice."
- RIGHT: "Charging the emergency segment its own price is **third-degree price discrimination**."
- RIGHT: "My input was time, so hourly billing was **cost-based pricing**."

The concept name should sit inside the analysis as a direct identification, the same way a physicist writes "the force on the block is friction" rather than "what we call friction acts on the block." A clarifying appositive giving an illustrative example is fine ("**third-degree price discrimination**, the same principle behind airline fares"). A clarifying appositive giving a textbook definition usually is not — the surrounding sentence has already done that work.

### Front-load comparison numbers

When a number anchors a comparison, put it next to the thing it compares against, not downstream. "Geek Squad charged $200 per hour" belongs in the sentence that introduces Geek Squad as the competitor, not a sentence later.

## When these rules do not apply

- Casual writing, chat messages, informal notes — use natural voice.
- Code comments, commit messages — different conventions apply.
- Direct quotation of a source — preserve the source's choices.
- Creative writing where rule-breaking is deliberate craft.

The rules above are tools for making careful prose read like a careful person wrote it. They are not a straitjacket. When breaking a rule produces better prose, break it — but know you are breaking it.
